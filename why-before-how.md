# Why Before How: A Different Way to Tackle Problems

When I was a junior engineer, I had one mode: solve it. A bug came in, I fixed it. A teammate flagged an edge case, I patched it. A stakeholder asked for a tweak, I shipped it. I wore "the person who fixes everything" as a badge of honor, and for a while, it worked — I was busy, useful, and always in demand.

But busy isn't the same as effective. Looking back, I was optimizing for the wrong thing.

## We're trained to jump to "how"

Most of us come out of school wired to solve problems the moment we see them. Assignments are handed to you already scoped and already important — your job is just to figure out the solution. That instinct carries into our first jobs, and it feels productive. Ticket comes in, you solve it. Repeat.

The trouble is that this trains us to skip a step that matters more than any of the engineering itself: understanding **why** the problem exists, and why it deserves your time *right now*.

## The question that changes everything

Before touching a solution, it's worth pausing on a few things:

- **Why does this problem matter?** What breaks, what's at risk, or what's not growing because this exists?
- **Why now?** What makes this more urgent than the ten other things competing for your attention?
- **Why you?** Are you actually the right owner, or is this someone else's fire that got handed to you because you're responsive?

These aren't rhetorical questions — they're the actual filter that separates important work from busywork. A problem that seems urgent when someone pings you directly often looks very different once you ask what it's actually blocking, and for whom.

## What changed for me

Early in my career, I said yes to almost everything. Every bug, every one-off request, every "can you just quickly look at this" — I treated all of it as equally worth solving, because solving things was the job, right?

The shift happened when I started forcing myself to ask *why* before I let myself think about *how*. Why is this the problem to solve today? Why does it matter more than the three other things on my plate? Once I started asking that consistently, the picture changed. A lot of what I was solving turned out to be noise — real, but not consequential. A smaller set of problems were the ones actually blocking outcomes that mattered: performance bottlenecks affecting real usage, gaps that were quietly costing time or money at scale, work that unblocked other people.

Narrowing down to that smaller set didn't mean doing less. It meant the work I did do had real, visible impact — instead of being spread thin across everything that happened to land in front of me. It also gave me something I didn't have before: the ability to push back. Once you can articulate *why* a problem matters and *why now*, you can just as easily articulate why something else doesn't meet that bar yet. That's a very different conversation than "I don't have time."

## The order that works

The instinct to fix things fast isn't wrong — it's just incomplete without sequencing. The order that's served me well since:

1. **Why** — why does this matter, why now, why is it prioritized over everything else competing for attention.
2. **What** — what does "solved" actually look like; what's the real scope of the problem once you understand its importance.
3. **How** — only now, with the why and what settled, does the actual engineering begin.

Most of us are taught to start at step 3. Starting at step 1 instead is a small shift, but it changes what you spend your time on — and eventually, it changes what people trust you to own.

---

*If you're early in your career and feel like you're constantly firefighting, try this: before you fix the next thing that lands on your desk, ask why it matters and why now. You might find, like I did, that not everything asking for your attention deserves it equally.*
