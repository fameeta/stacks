# Self-Advocacy Is an Engineering Skill Too

*This article was written with the help of AI.*

Somewhere along the way, a lot of engineers pick up an unspoken rule: do great work, stay quiet, and let the work speak for itself. It sounds humble. It sounds like the "right" way to be a good engineer. But a few years into a senior career, I've come to believe it's one of the more limiting habits we carry — and one worth unlearning deliberately.

## Good work that nobody hears about isn't done yet

An optimization that cuts compute cost 90%. A refactor that removes thousands of lines of fragile code. A fix that shaves real latency off a critical path. A tweak that saves every user a handful of clicks. These are wins — but a win that lives only in a commit history or a closed ticket has done half its job. The other half is making sure the right people know it happened, why it mattered, and what it unlocked.

That's what self-advocacy actually is. Not self-promotion for its own sake — communicating impact, deliberately and specifically, so the value of the work compounds beyond the one PR or one team.

## It's not bragging — it's data

The discomfort a lot of us feel here usually comes from thinking of this as bragging. It isn't. A well-written launch note or impact summary is just data: what changed, by how much, and why it matters. That data is genuinely useful to other people — a team hunting for a template to solve a similar problem, a manager building the case for headcount, a peer team facing the same bottleneck you just removed.

Impact isn't limited to one flavor either. It can show up as:

- **Cost savings** — infra spend, compute hours, GPU utilization
- **Performance** — latency, memory footprint, throughput
- **Simplification** — lines of code removed, systems retired, complexity reduced
- **Developer productivity** — hours saved, build times, debugging time cut
- **Operational health** — fewer on-call pages, faster incident resolution, more reliable pipelines
- **User experience** — fewer clicks, faster load times, smoother flows

None of these need to map directly to a team OKR to be worth sharing. Some of the most useful wins are the ones that show up sideways — a debugging tool you built for yourself that three other engineers end up using, or a small operational fix that quietly removes a recurring fire drill.

## The channels matter more than we think

Sharing doesn't have to mean a big stage. It can be:

- A launch announcement to your org with the before/after numbers
- An internal blog post or tech talk walking through the problem and the fix
- A conference talk or external publication, if the work has broader relevance
- Even just a well-written update in a team channel that puts real numbers next to the work

The format matters less than the habit. What matters is that the impact gets written down, with numbers, in a place where people beyond your immediate team can find it.

## It sharpens how you pick your next problem

There's a second-order benefit that's easy to miss: writing up impact regularly changes how you *look* for problems. Once you've felt what it's like to quantify a win — this saved $X, cut latency by Y%, removed Z hours of manual work — you start noticing similar shapes elsewhere. You start working backward from "what would a strong impact story look like here" when you're scoping new work, which naturally pulls you toward problems that matter more, not just problems that are in front of you.

It becomes a filter, not just a recap.

## The fear is the biggest blocker

The honest barrier for most of us isn't lack of impact — it's the discomfort of putting our name on something visible. That first announcement, that first internal talk, feels disproportionately hard. But it gets easier every time, and the payoff compounds: once you're known for shipping and communicating meaningful work, you start getting pulled into more of it. More ownership, more interesting problems, more trust to run with ambiguous work — all downstream of people simply *knowing* what you've done.

Self-advocacy isn't a personality trait some engineers have and others don't. It's a skill, and like any skill, it gets easier with reps. The first write-up is the hardest one. After that, it's just part of how you work.

---

*If you've been sitting on a win — big or small — that never made it past a closed ticket, that's a good place to start. Write down what changed, put a number on it, and share it somewhere more people can see.*
